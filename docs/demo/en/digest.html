    6 tips for better sleep | Sleeping with Science, a TED series

    TED Sleep produced 2026-10-01 🔗 Watch on YouTube

    P procedural→ PracticeHow-to information: use it in a real context as early as possible; cramming is wasted.

    Fix one bed time and one wake time, and defend them on weekends too.
    1. Pick the wake time first — it is the one you can actually enforce
    2. Count back the hours you need to get the bed time
    3. Keep both within about 30 minutes, weekday or weekend
    4. Hold the wake time even after a bad night; do not sleep in to repay it
    Do today Open your calendar now and put one fixed wake time on all seven days of next week, including Saturday and Sunday.
    Segment 2 → Tip 1: regularity, weekends included
    Set a to-bed alarm, the half of the alarm nobody sets.
    1. Set a recurring alarm 30–60 minutes before your target bed time
    2. Label it with the first action of your wind-down, not the word 'bed'
    3. When it fires, stop what you are doing even if unfinished
    Do today Set that to-bed alarm on your phone today and label it with one concrete action, e.g. 'put the laptop in the drawer'.
    Segment 2 → Tip 1: regularity, weekends included
    Lower the bedroom instead of adding blankets.
    1. Measure the actual bedroom temperature at bed time for three nights
    2. Move it toward 18 °C with a window, a fan, or the thermostat
    3. Keep extremities warm if that feels too cold — the core is what must fall
    Do today Put a thermometer in your bedroom tonight and write down the number at lights-out; decide tomorrow whether it needs to come down.
    Segment 3 → Tip 2: keep it cool
    Dim the house in the last hour, not just the phone.
    1. One hour before bed, switch off half the lights in the house
    2. Prefer low lamps at eye level over overhead ceiling lights
    3. Then deal with screens; add an eye mask or blackout shades if light leaks in
    Do today Tonight, turn off the overhead light in whichever room you spend the last hour in and use a single lamp instead.
    Segment 4 → Tip 3: darkness, starting in the evening
    Leave the bed when sleep will not come, and come back only when sleepy.
    1. Notice you have been lying awake for roughly 20–25 minutes
    2. Get up and leave the bedroom
    3. Do something quiet in dim light — not screens, not work
    4. Return only when you feel sleepy, not merely when you feel tired of waiting
    Do today Decide tonight, before you get in, exactly what you will go and do if you are still awake after 20 minutes — and put that thing where you will find it.
    Segment 5 → Tip 4: walk it out
    Cut the caffeine deadline, and stop treating a nightcap as help.
    1. Set a daily caffeine cut-off in the early afternoon
    2. Count the hidden sources too — tea, cola, dark chocolate, some painkillers
    3. Keep alcohol away from the last hours before bed
    Do today Write down every caffeinated thing you drank today with its time; if anything lands after 2 pm, move it earlier tomorrow.
    Segment 6 → Tip 5: alcohol and caffeine
    Build one wind-down routine and then stop redesigning it.
    1. Reserve the last 20–60 minutes before bed
    2. Disengage from computer and phone at the start of that window
    3. Do the same relaxing thing each night — reading, stretching, a shower
    4. Once something works, keep it; the repetition is part of the mechanism
    Do today Choose one thing you will do for the last 20 minutes tonight, and do the identical thing tomorrow and the night after.
    Segment 7 → Tip 6: sleep is a landing, not a switch

    A analogous→ CritiqueInformation that resembles what you know: find where it matches, where it doesn't, and when it breaks, so new knowledge attaches to the old network.

    Melatonin is a starting gun, not the race — it says when, not how.
    new melatonin as a timing signal for sleep  ⇄  like a starting gun at a race: it sets the moment things begin but does nothing to run the race
    Where alike Both are signals that start something without doing any of the work, and both can fire at the wrong time.
    Where different A starting gun is one discrete bang; melatonin rises over hours and stays up all night, so late light does not just delay the gun, it flattens the whole curve.
    When it breaks Completely, for anyone taking melatonin as a supplement expecting to be knocked out. It schedules sleep; it does not sedate.
    Segment 4 → Tip 3: darkness, starting in the evening
    Walker's own framing: the bed is a trigger the brain has been trained on.
    new lying awake in bed teaching the brain that bed means wakefulness  ⇄  like a dog learning that a particular sound means food — the cue acquires the meaning through repetition
    Where alike Both are repeated pairings of a context with a state, and both weaken once the pairing stops.
    Where different Classical conditioning needs a biologically significant outcome; the bed association is closer to contextual learning. The fix is not extinguishing a reflex but withdrawing the context — which is exactly what leaving the bed does.
    When it breaks For someone kept awake by pain or apnea: re-pairing cannot fix a cue that was never the cause.
    Segment 5 → Tip 4: walk it out
    The talk's best image: falling asleep is landing a plane, not flipping a switch.
    new sleep onset as a gradual physiological descent  ⇄  like an aircraft descending over many minutes onto a runway rather than stopping instantly
    Where alike Both take real time, both need a prepared approach, and both go wrong if you try to arrive too steeply.
    Where different A landing is actively flown and can be aborted and retried; sleep onset is the one thing that resists effort — trying harder reliably makes it worse.
    When it breaks The video's own animation quietly admits it: the viewer is at the terminal window watching, not in the cockpit.
    Segment 7 → Tip 6: sleep is a landing, not a switch
    Walker's boundary: coaching cannot fix a broken ankle.
    new sleep tips not helping someone with insomnia or apnea  ⇄  like a sports coach's performance advice being useless to an athlete with a broken ankle until the ankle is treated
    Where alike Both separate optimisation from repair, and both insist on the order: treat first, optimise after.
    Where different A broken ankle is visible and unambiguous; insomnia is self-reported and shades into ordinary bad sleep, so the clean either/or hides the hard part — knowing which side you are on.
    When it breaks It undersells the overlap: stimulus control, tip four here, is itself a component of CBT-I. Some of the 'coaching' is the treatment.
